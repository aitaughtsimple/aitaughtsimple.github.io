What we teach

Practical, everyday uses. No tech words and no pressure.

  • Write emails and letters

    Get help with a note to the doctor's office, a letter to your landlord, or a reply to family.

    Try asking:"Help me write a polite email asking to move my appointment to next week."

  • Get quick answers

    Ask a question in plain words and get a clear answer, without digging through websites.

    Try asking:"How do I make the text bigger on my phone?"

  • Plan trips and meals

    Make a packing list, plan a day trip, or turn what's in your fridge into dinner ideas.

    Try asking:"I have chicken, rice, and spinach. What can I make for two?"

  • Spot scams and stay safe

    Learn how fake calls, voice clones, and phishing texts work, and which personal info to keep to yourself.

    A good rule:A call from family asking for money? Hang up and call them back on a number you know.

How a class works

Three simple steps. Go at your own pace.

  1. 1

    Bring your phone

    iPhone or Android. Come as you are. No experience needed, and no laptop.

  2. 2

    Send your first message

    In the first few minutes, a student helper sits with you while you ask the AI your very first question. You can't break anything.

  3. 3

    Practice what matters to you

    We show one task slowly on the big screen. Then you try it yourself, at your own pace, and finish with a quick scam-spotting practice.

Every class is free and takes about an hour. Most of that hour is hands-on.

Real or scam? Try it

Eight messages like the ones people really get. Some are scams and some are real, so take your time. Pick an answer to see why.

  1. Text message

    USPS: Your package can't be delivered because of an incomplete address. Update it within 24 hours: usps-redeliver-help.example

    This is a scam.

    USPS only texts you if you signed up for tracking with a tracking number, and even then the text has no link. This address isn't usps.com, and the 24-hour deadline is meant to rush you. Forward texts like this to 7726, then delete them.

  2. Email from your library

    "Reminder: The book you borrowed is due Friday. You can renew it in your library account." It has no links and asks for nothing.

    This one is real.

    It doesn't rush you, ask for money, or ask for a password. When in doubt, go to the library's website yourself instead of clicking.

  3. Phone call

    A voice that sounds just like your grandson, crying: "Grandma, I'm in trouble. Please send $2,000 in gift cards, and don't tell Mom."

    This is a scam.

    Scammers can copy a voice from a short video online, and caller ID can be faked too. Gift cards and secrecy are red flags. Hang up and call him on the number you already have.

  4. Pop-up on your computer

    "WARNING! Your computer has a virus. Call Microsoft Support now at the number below. Do not turn off your computer."

    This is a scam.

    The FTC says real security pop-up warnings never ask you to call a phone number. Don't call. Close the window, and if it won't close, ask someone you trust for help.

  5. Text message you asked for

    You just tapped "Text me a code" to sign in to your email. A few seconds later: "Your sign-in code is 482913. Don't share this code with anyone."

    This one is real.

    You asked for it seconds ago, and you type the code in yourself. Never read a code like this to anyone who calls or texts asking for it, even if they say they're from your bank. That's a common trick.

  6. Text from your doctor's office

    "Reminder: You have an appointment on Tuesday at 10:30 AM. Reply C to confirm." You do have that appointment, and the text has no link.

    This one is real.

    You know about the appointment, there's no link, and it doesn't ask for money or private details. If a reminder ever asks you to pay or to share your insurance or Medicare number, call the office using the number on your appointment card or bill.

  7. Text message

    "Unpaid toll: $6.99. Pay today to avoid a $50 late fee: ezpass-tollpay.example"

    This is a scam.

    Maryland never texts people about unpaid tolls. To check your account, type DriveEzMD.com into your browser yourself. In another state, go to that state's own toll website. Never use the link in the text.

  8. In the ChatGPT app

    You ask for an easy dinner idea, and ChatGPT suggests a recipe. It doesn't ask you for anything personal.

    This one is real.

    That's a normal answer. Never type your Social Security number, bank or card numbers, or passwords into an AI chat. A real AI assistant doesn't need them to help you. If a chat or app asks, stop and ask someone you trust.

Think you've been scammed? Do this now

  1. Stop. Don't send any more money or share any more information.
  2. Call your bank or card company using the number on the back of your card or on your statement.
  3. Report it at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and ic3.gov.
  4. Tell someone you trust. Scams fool smart, careful people every day. It's not your fault.

Watch out for a second trick: anyone who contacts you offering to get your money back for a fee is another scammer.

Questions

Is it really free?

Yes. Classes are free for everyone who comes and for the library or center that hosts them. We will never ask you for money, bank details, or a card number. If someone using our name does, it's a scam.

Do I need a laptop?

No. Just bring your smartphone. iPhone and Android both work, and a tablet is fine too.

Who can come?

Anyone who wants to learn. Our classes are made with older adults in mind, and no experience is needed.

Is AI safe to use?

AI tools can be very helpful, and they're safest when you know a few simple rules. AI can get things wrong, and scammers use it too. In class, we show you how to:

  • Keep passwords, bank details, and your Social Security number out of AI chats
  • Double-check important answers, especially about health and money
  • Spot AI scams like fake calls, cloned voices, and phishing texts

Do I need to download an app or make an account?

Usually not. Many phones already have an AI assistant built in, and ChatGPT's website often works without signing up. If you'd like to add an app, you type your own password. We never ask for it.

What if I don't have a smartphone?

Come anyway. You can follow along on the big screen and practice on our demo phone with a helper.

Can you fix my phone or computer?

We're not a repair service. During class, we're glad to help with simple settings, like making the text bigger or turning on voice typing.

Where can I find a longer course?

Howard County's Office on Aging and Independence offers technology classes with Senior Planet. See the county's digital education page. We're a separate student club, not part of that program.
